What Are Fossils?

Definition

A fossil is preserved evidence of past life. Fossils can be physical remains, such as bones, shells, teeth, and wood, or indirect evidence, such as tracks, burrows, and chemical signatures.

The word comes from the Latin fossilis, meaning “dug up.”

Why Fossils Matter to Science

  • Evolutionary record: Fossils provide physical evidence of organisms that no longer exist.
  • Biostratigraphy: Fossil assemblages help geologists compare and date rock layers.
  • Paleoecology: Fossils reveal ancient food webs, climate zones, and ecosystems.
  • Paleoclimatology: Fossils such as pollen and foraminifera help reconstruct past climates.

Which Organisms Become Fossils?

Fossilization is biased toward organisms with hard parts, such as bone, shell, teeth, wood, or chitin, because soft tissues decay quickly. Marine invertebrates, vertebrates, plants, and microscopic organisms are common in the fossil record.

Types of Fossils

Fossils come in many forms depending on which parts of an organism were preserved and by what process.

Body Fossils

Preserved physical remains of an organism, including bones, teeth, shells, wood, and pollen.

Trace Fossils

Preserved evidence of behavior rather than the body itself, such as footprints, burrows, feeding marks, nests, and coprolites.

Mold and Cast Fossils

A mold is a negative impression left after the original organism dissolves. A cast forms when that mold fills with sediment or minerals.

Compression Fossils

These form when an organism is buried and squeezed, often leaving a thin carbon film or flat impression.

Permineralized Fossils

Mineral-rich groundwater fills tiny spaces in bones or wood, preserving the original shape and sometimes fine details.

Amber Fossils

Small organisms can become trapped in tree resin, which hardens into amber over millions of years.

Formation Process

Fossilization, formally studied as taphonomy, is rare. Several conditions must happen in the right order for an organism to become preserved.

1. Death and Exposure

An organism dies and begins to face scavenging, decomposition, and physical damage.

2. Rapid Burial

Sediment covers the remains, reducing oxygen exposure and slowing decay. This is one of the most important steps.

3. Soft Tissue Decay

Most soft tissue breaks down, while hard parts such as bones, shells, and teeth are more likely to survive.

4. Mineralization

Groundwater carrying minerals fills spaces in the remains or replaces the original material over time.

5. Lithification

The surrounding sediment compacts and cements into rock, preserving the fossil inside.

6. Uplift, Erosion, and Discovery

Tectonic uplift and erosion expose the rock layers. Eventually, a fossil may be discovered by scientists or fossil hunters.

Famous Discoveries

Some fossil discoveries changed how scientists understand evolution and Earth history.

Archaeopteryx lithographica — The First Bird

Bavaria, Germany • First recognized 1861

Archaeopteryx had both dinosaur-like and bird-like features, making it important evidence for the evolutionary link between dinosaurs and birds.

Lucy — Australopithecus afarensis

Hadar, Ethiopia • 1974

Lucy showed that upright walking developed before large brain size in human evolution.

Tiktaalik roseae — The Fish with Shoulders

Ellesmere Island, Arctic Canada • Published 2006

Tiktaalik is a transitional fossil between fish and early four-limbed vertebrates.

Sue the T. rex

Hell Creek Formation, South Dakota • 1990

Sue is one of the largest and most complete Tyrannosaurus rex skeletons ever found.
